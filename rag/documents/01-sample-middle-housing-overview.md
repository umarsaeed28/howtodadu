## What "middle housing" means

Middle housing refers to house-scale buildings with more than one home on a
lot: duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, townhouses, cottage clusters, and
stacked flats. Washington's House Bill 1110 (HB 1110) requires cities like
Seattle to allow more of these types in areas that were formerly limited to
single detached houses.

## Units allowed per lot

- Most residential lots: up to **four homes**.
- Lots within a **quarter mile of frequent transit**: up to **six homes**.
- Lots that include affordable homes may qualify for an additional unit under
  some programs.

## Neighborhood Residential (NR) zones

Seattle's Neighborhood Residential zones (NR1, NR2, NR3) set the base
development standards. Typical constraints include:

- Floor area ratio (FAR) limits by zone and by number of units.
- Maximum structure height (commonly around 32 feet for pitched-roof forms).
- Required rear/side yard setbacks and lot coverage limits.

## Accessory dwelling units (ADU / DADU)

An attached ADU (AADU) or detached ADU (DADU / backyard cottage) can add homes
on a lot in addition to the primary structure. DADUs have their own height,
size, and setback rules, and can often be combined with middle-housing unit
counts.

## Trees and environmentally critical areas

Tree protection rules and Environmentally Critical Areas (ECA) — such as steep
slopes, wetlands, or riparian buffers — can reduce the buildable area and the
net lot size used to compute allowed floor area.
